# Choosing Cookware That Supports a Healing Kitchen

The pots and pans you cook in matter almost as much as what goes into them. Here's a look at common cookware materials, plus what to weigh before you buy.

## Cookware Materials at a Glance

**Stainless Steel**
A kitchen staple for a reason — it resists scratching and corrosion, and its inert surface means it generally won't react with acidic foods (tomatoes, citrus, vinegar-based dishes) or leach into your meals. Look for 18/8 or 18/10 grade for the most reliable quality.

**Cast Iron**
Naturally non-stick once seasoned, and durable enough to move from stovetop to oven. Research on iron cookware and iron deficiency (including a 2019 systematic review in *PLOS ONE*) suggests it *may* modestly increase the iron content of food, especially acidic dishes cooked longer — but the evidence is mixed, not settled, so treat it as a possible bonus rather than a guaranteed iron boost. If you're managing iron levels specifically, that's a conversation for your healthcare provider.

**Enamel-Coated Cast Iron**
Combines cast iron's heat retention with a coated surface that doesn't require seasoning and won't react with acidic ingredients. A solid middle ground if you want cast-iron performance without the upkeep.

**Ceramic (100% Pure)**
Made from natural materials with no metal or synthetic coating, so there's minimal concern about leaching. It heats evenly but is breakable and tends to cost more. If you go this route, confirm any glaze is explicitly labeled lead-free and cadmium-free.

**Glass**
Completely non-reactive and lets you watch food cook without lifting a lid. Reliable for standard baking temperatures, but avoid sudden temperature swings (freezer to oven, or hot glass into cold water) — that's what causes cracking, not the cooking heat itself.

**Titanium**
Light and durable, often sold with a non-stick surface. Titanium alloys are commonly used in medical devices, which speaks to general biocompatibility, though that's not the same as a direct safety study on cookware — the coating and manufacturing matter more than the base metal.

**Carbon Steel**
Heats fast and, like cast iron, builds a natural non-stick layer through seasoning over time. A favorite for high-heat cooking that needs quick, even temperature control.

**Silicone Bakeware**
Naturally non-stick and flexible, making it easy on baked goods and easy to clean. Choose food-grade silicone from a reputable brand — quality varies more than with rigid cookware.

## What to Actually Weigh When Choosing

Most people start with the visible stuff — how it looks, the size, the weight, how long it'll last, how easy it is to clean, and the price. All reasonable. But for a healing-focused kitchen, run it through two extra filters before you buy:

- **What is it actually made of?** Some materials and coatings can carry toxic elements — knowing what's in your cookware matters as much as what you cook in it.
- **Is there a coating or glaze, and is it something you'd want touching your food at high heat?** Non-stick coatings and glazes vary widely in quality and safety, so this is worth checking per-product rather than assuming.
